# Bayview reference report

Research for the Bayview rebuild: a bold, brightly painted harbour town on a ~300 x 240 m island, joined to the mainland ~1 km away by a causeway/bridge. Written 19 Oct 2026.

How to read this: "Source" figures were found on the cited pages. "Estimate" figures are my own design guidance (not sourced) and need checking against the game's scale. Web search found very few real harbour dimensions; most of the sizes below are therefore estimates and are labelled so.

## 1. Real places and what makes them recognisable

**Tobermory, Mull** (the Balamory town). Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobermory,_Mull ; https://www.undiscoveredscotland.co.uk/mull/tobermory/index.html
- One continuous curve of brightly painted Main Street frontages (pink, yellow, blue, red) facing the water. Main Street was made by cutting back the cliff and reclaiming land, so the row is long and thin, with the hill rising straight behind it.
- Clock tower on the harbour wall (a memorial gift), the single landmark.
- A sheltered natural bay protected by a long offshore island (Calve Island); town in two levels (lower street, upper town on the hill).
- Pier used as the viewpoint over the street; ferry and tour boats.
- Population 1,045 (2022), so a compact, walkable centre.

**St Ives, Cornwall.** Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Ives,_Cornwall ; https://thetravelbunny.com/best-things-to-do-st-ives-cornwall/
- Smeaton's Pier (rebuilt 1766-70) curving round the harbour, with an octagonal cupola lookout and a lighthouse on its end; a second lighthouse was added in the 1890s extension. Length is quoted inconsistently (one source says 600 ft today).
- Harbour dries at low tide: boats sit on sand. About 140 moorings.
- Lifeboat station at the landward end of the West Pier (1993 building); earlier boathouses on Fore Street and the Quay.
- Old "Downalong" streets are narrow and uneven; newer streets on rising ground are wider. Granite and slate cottages stack up the slope.
- Beaches on both sides of the headland (Porthminster is almost half a mile of sand).

**Mousehole, Cornwall.** Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mousehole ; https://eoceanic.com/sailing/harbours/568/ ; https://www.iwalkcornwall.co.uk/pi/mousehole_harbour
- A tiny harbour enclosed by two granite arms with a narrow entrance of about 11-12 m (source).
- Timber beams (baulks) are laid across the entrance each winter as a storm gate (source: https://theninebritishart.co.uk/artists/jeremy-gardiner/works/920/).
- Granite quay faces; the south pier is large blocks and rubble (17th century, extended in the 19th).
- Cottages crowd right to the quay edge; whitewashed rubble walls and slate roofs.
- St Clement's Isle about 350 m offshore (source); Christmas lights around the harbour. Both are good "one memorable thing" ideas.

**Port Isaac, Cornwall.** Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_Isaac
- A steep, narrow cleft with cottages stacked down a valley to a small tidal harbour.
- Temple Bar ("Squeezibelly Alley") is 18 inches (about 0.46 m) wide at its narrowest. The 1978 Guinness Book of Records called it the world's narrowest thoroughfare.
- Fish cellars along the Platt (the quay-side area), a Tudor-era pier, about 90 listed buildings.
- Slipway on the beach beside the harbour (https://explore.predictwind.com/marinas/united-kingdom/england/port-isaac-harbour).
- Whitewash and slate roofs, but colour comes from door and boat paint.

**Portmeirion, Wales.** Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clough_Williams-Ellis ; https://www.AtlasObscura.com/places/portmeirion-village ; https://www.solosophie.com/portmeirion-wales/
- Pastel palette (pink, yellow, blue, ochre) on a steep site above the Dwyryd estuary, best seen from the water.
- Buildings arranged around a central piazza/pond and lawn; many incorporate salvaged fragments ("a home for fallen buildings").
- Italianate shapes (loggias, domes, towers) mixed with other styles; the colour is the Italian bit, not the architecture.
- Stepped, terraced layout so every house has a view and a roof you can read from below.

**Portofino and Cinque Terre, Italy.** Source: https://www.summerinitaly.com/guide/portofino ; https://choosewhere.com/portofino-visitor-guide ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinque_Terre
- Portofino: a small horseshoe harbour with a piazzetta. Tall, narrow houses in yellow, pink, orange and green with painted-on stonework and shutters. Castello Brown above, and a lighthouse on the Punta del Capo headland. The harbour takes a few minutes to walk round.
- Cinque Terre: pastel houses climb steep terraces and fill a gully. Vernazza has the natural harbour; at Riomaggiore boats are pulled up a ramp and kayaks crowd the slipway; Manarola has a tiny harbour under the houses. Vernazza has a castle ruin as a viewpoint.

**Nyhavn, Copenhagen.** Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nyhavn
- A canal about 450 m long (source), dug 1670-75, lined with narrow tall townhouses in strong colours (17th and early 18th century).
- Veteran wooden ships moored on the canal; museum ships on the south side.
- Open quay on one side for cafes, houses on the other: the "colour wall" reads in a single glance.
- Gabled, steep rooflines, each house one colour.

**St Michael's Mount and Lindisfarne (tidal and causeway islands).**
- St Michael's Mount. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Michael%27s_Mount ; https://www.britannica.com/print/article/517887 : island of 0.23 km2 about 400 yd (365 m) to 500 m offshore. Cobbled granite-sett causeway passable from mid tide to low water; harbour with a 15th-century pier, widened in 1823. Castle on the summit; a village of 53 houses and four streets by 1811 (Elizabeth Terrace of eight houses added in 1885). Tidal range up to about 5 m. The whole island is one tiered silhouette.
- Lindisfarne. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindisfarne ; https://visitnorthumberland.com/coast/holy-island/crossing-times : about 3 x 1.5 miles; nearest mainland point about 0.8 mile (1.3 km). The metalled road dates from 1954; it floods fast and can be under up to 5 m of water (https://www.ciht.org.uk/media/8063/managing_the_holy_island_causeway.pdf). Raised refuge box on stilts (the current box replaced one built in 1954: https://www.berwick-advertiser.co.uk/news/people/holy-island-refuge-box-replaced-after-damage-caused-by-storm-arwen-4130472). Castle on a whinstone hill (Beblowe), lime kilns, and upturned old boats used as sheds on the foreshore. The causeway length was not stated in any page I read.

### Dimension estimates for the game (estimates, not sourced)
- Quay: about 5-6 m wide in the game for a car to pass parked boxes; quay wall 2.5-3.5 m above water for a tidal harbour (comparison points found: a Scottish pier edge sits about 3.4 m above high spring water, https://marine.gov.scot/sites/default/files/phase_2_pier_cross_section_plan.pdf; bollards on a commercial quay about 21.5 m apart, https://www.peelports.com/media/okhjyrdh/berth-data-sheet-great-harbour-v5-jul-22.pdf).
- Harbour mouth: 11-12 m (Mousehole is the real anchor).
- Slipway gradient: 1 in 5 to 1 in 10, lifeboat ones about 1 in 5-6 (source: https://lifeboatmagazinearchive.rnli.org/volume/26/287/launching-life-boats-off-slipways?page=505).
- Houses: 5-7 m wide frontages, 2-3 storeys (about 7-10 m to eaves) so streets of 5-6 m read as intimate. Nyhavn and Portofino go taller and narrower.
- Streets: main road 6-7 m (two lanes), harbour street 5 m, back lanes 3.5-4 m (one car), footpath/steps 1-2 m. Port Isaac's 0.46 m alley is a cut-through for the player on foot or a joke gap, not a road.
- Causeway: 1 km sits between St Michael's Mount's gap (0.4-0.5 km) and Lindisfarne's 1.3 km nearest-mainland distance; a raised "refuge box" or lay-by halfway gives a landmark.

## 2. Street furniture checklist

Sources are general: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helter_skelter_(ride) , https://www.britannica.com/technology/lobster-pot , https://www.seafish.org/responsible-sourcing/fishing-gear-database/gear/pots-and-traps-lobster/ ; the rest is general knowledge (not individually sourced).

- Capstan: waist-high iron drum on the quay used to winch boats and ropes; round, easy to model, good collision.
- Bollards: short iron posts along the quay edge for ropes; use as a rhythm of obstacles (comparison: 20 m or so apart on commercial quays, estimate 10 m for a small harbour; old fishing quays used ring bolts at about 10 ft / 3 m spacing: https://www.kbha.co.za/BULLETINS/18/files/basic-html/page77.html).
- Lobster pots / creels: stacks of rectangular pots (about 76-102 cm long) with rope and floats; classic quay clutter that can be scattered by a car.
- Net shed / net loft / fish cellar: tall slate-roofed or tar-black timber sheds on the quay, often with a hoist beam and loading door.
- Upturned boat sheds: old hulls turned over as roofs (Lindisfarne).
- Harbourmaster's office: small hut with signal mast, tide board, flag and lifebelt.
- Lifeboat station and slipway: big double doors on a steep, wide slipway into the water; the strongest "this is a rescue town" shape.
- Tide gauge / tide board: a tall painted staff on the quay wall with numbered marks.
- Lighthouse and harbour lights: tower at the pier end, with red and green lights at the harbour mouth.
- Fish stalls, crab shack, chip shop, ice-cream kiosk: bright kiosks for colour.
- Pier with a helter-skelter: lighthouse-shaped spiral slide (first at Blackpool Pleasure Beach in 1906; ~15 m / 50 ft versions exist), plus amusements and a kiosk.
- Bandstand: octagonal Victorian roofed stage on the front.
- Beach huts: rows of small coloured timber huts with a pitched or flat roof.
- Boat park / dinghy racks, fuel pump, ice plant, crane or derrick, chandlery.
- Fishing nets on drying poles, buoys, anchors as monuments, lifebelts and ladders in the quay wall.
- Steps and handrails cut into the quay wall; mooring ladders.
- Clock tower (Tobermory), cupola lookout (St Ives), castle or chapel on the summit (St Michael's Mount).
- Wooden boats on the hard, hauled-up dinghies, painted rowing boats on the slipway (Riomaggiore).

## 3. How games design starting areas

Evidence quality is mixed: Forza, Zelda, Pokemon and Disney are sourced; Mario Kart, CTR and Spyro are only partly covered by the pages I found, so treat those lines as indicative.

- Contained area, memorable exit: Zelda's Great Plateau teaches mechanics through exploration and ends when you leave with the paraglider (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Plateau). Lego 2K Drive starts in a "smallish" Turbo Acres that teaches the vehicle modes before the rest of the map (https://gameinformer.com/review/lego-2k-drive/stud-your-engines ; https://racinggames.gg/article/lego-2k-drive-map-every-biome-in-bricklandia). Spyro's Artisans is a hub with harmless enemies so you can practise, with portals to the next world (https://strategywiki.org/wiki/Spyro_the_Dragon/Artisans). For Bayview, the causeway is the "exit gate".
- Landmark visible from everywhere: Disney's "weenie" (castle) draws people towards it, then hides and reveals it from new angles (https://craphound.com/disneylandleveldesign.txt ; https://tancoque.design.blog/). A lighthouse and a clock tower give you both.
- Teach one thing at a time, disguised as story: Forza Horizon's intro lets you drive a chase to re-learn controls and meet traffic, with little text (https://www.thatgamesux.com/forza-horizon-one-amazing-tutorial ; https://shacknews.com/article/86565/getting-started-with-forza-horizon-2). FH5's director says the goal is to treat players "with respect" and not bog them down in tutorials (https://grandavehousing.calpoly.edu/news/conquer-forza-horizon-5-a). Early map access is restricted to steer you to set events (https://www.supercheats.com/forza-horizon/walkthrough/part-1-the-wristband-races).
- Easy first course: Mario Kart Wii's Luigi Circuit is the shortest introductory course with no hazards and one big banked boost turn before the finish (https://www.mariowiki.com/Wii_Luigi_Circuit).
- Avoid a hard open map at first: Burnout Paradise's reviewers disliked a long tutorial, confusing menus and a big city with events at every junction when you only have a minimap (https://www.gamespot.com/reviews/burnout-paradise-review/1900-6185049/ ; https://www.engadget.com/2007-09-27-joystiq-hands-on-burnout-paradise.html). Bayview's small size is an advantage.
- Safe first wins: Pokemon's Route 1 offers a free Potion, weak wild creatures and winding grass patches that feel longer than they are, with one-way ledges to make backtracking easy (https://bulbapedia.bulbagarden.net/wiki/Kanto_Route_1 ; https://fantendo.fandom.com/wiki/User_blog:Shadow_Inferno/A_short_analysis_of_the_first_Routes_in_Pokemon_Games_(Gen_I-IV)).
- Hub: one place (town square, garage, shop) that everything returns to, with more destinations visible as you get there.

Principles for Bayview: (1) one big landmark you can see from the whole island and the causeway; (2) roads that each teach one skill; (3) a first race you cannot lose badly; (4) a free early reward; (5) a clear exit that is also a reveal of the wider world; (6) a hub (the Pit Stop/garage) at the heart.

## 4. Short island circuit (~1-1.5 km lap)

Note: an island of 300 x 240 m has a coastline of roughly 1 km, so a 1-1.5 km lap needs the circuit to fold inwards, use two levels, or run out along the causeway or pier. Working it into the town streets and up the hill is the likely answer.

Corner vocabulary from coaching sources (https://www.motorsportprospects.com/the-7-technical-corner-types-in-racing/ ; https://kartclass.com/blogs/track-guides/types-of-corners-in-racing): hairpin, chicane, esses, constant radius, increasing radius, decreasing radius (tightens, traps late brakers), and sweepers that tighten or open.

Suggested teaching order (my proposal, not sourced):
1. Harbour-front straight (start/finish, 200-250 m): full throttle, quay on one side and painted houses on the other; teaches speed and braking marker with a clock tower at the end.
2. Gentle constant-radius right-hander round the harbour arm: the first "lift and steer" corner, wide and run-off friendly.
3. Climb through the town (about 5-10% grade, steps and cobbles): teaches elevation and a crest; a decreasing-radius left through the old streets.
4. Esses between houses (2-3 quick direction changes, 5-6 m road): a line-choice lesson, narrow enough to feel quick but with soft edges.
5. Lighthouse headland: an open sweeper that tightens, with a drop on the outside and a view; teaches speed on exposed ground.
6. Hairpin (180 degrees) at the headland tip or top of the hill, with a long run-up so the braking point is obvious; then a downhill run back.
7. Gravel or sand shortcut across the beach or slipway as an optional line (an "early win" for a bold player), rejoining before the harbour-front straight.
8. Optional spur: causeway run (about 1 km) as a time-trial, sprint or later loop extension.

Keep the track 7-8 m wide (two cars side by side), narrowing to 5-6 m in the old streets. Make sure the lighthouse is visible from the start line, and keep every landmark in a different direction so it works as a compass.
